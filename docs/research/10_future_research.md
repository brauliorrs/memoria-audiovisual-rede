# Future Research

## Purpose

The infrastructure was created to support research questions that cannot be answered by a single static inventory. Its main long-term value lies in the accumulation of comparable, provenance-rich observations across time.

The directions below are research possibilities, not claims that the required evidence has already been collected.

## Longitudinal access research

Repeated observation can examine whether eligible audiovisual archives are becoming more open or more restrictive over time.

Possible questions include:

- Which institutions remove registration or formal-request barriers?
- Which institutions introduce authentication, payment, or territorial restrictions?
- Are changes temporary, recurrent, or persistent?
- Do access patterns differ by country, institution type, or platform model?

The Audiovisual Archive Access Index provides a simple archive-level measure, while event histories preserve the institutional changes behind the aggregate value.

## Interoperability diffusion

The platform can support studies of how standards spread across archival ecosystems.

Potential analyses include:

- adoption trajectories of IIIF and OAI-PMH;
- diffusion of Dublin Core, Schema.org, and JSON-LD;
- relationships between APIs and interoperability mechanisms;
- differences between aggregators and individual archives;
- concentration around particular technical standards.

Longitudinal comparison is essential because a standard detected once may later disappear, migrate, or become inaccessible.

## Digital preservation and infrastructural stability

Observable continuity and change can be used to investigate infrastructural stability without claiming access to internal preservation systems.

Potential measures include:

- recurrence of unavailable routes;
- disappearance of public resources;
- changes in identifiers or delivery mechanisms;
- platform migrations;
- persistence of metadata and access surfaces;
- frequency of material technological change.

These observations should be interpreted as evidence about public digital infrastructure, not as a complete audit of institutional preservation capacity.

## Artificial intelligence governance

The current infrastructure records publicly observable AI-related signals conservatively. Future research may expand this into a documented governance framework.

Possible questions include:

- Where is AI use publicly declared?
- Which tasks are associated with AI, such as transcription, classification, recognition, or recommendation?
- Are purposes, limitations, providers, and oversight arrangements documented?
- Does AI adoption increase together with transparency?
- How do declared practices change over time?

Absence of public evidence must remain distinguishable from confirmed absence of AI use.

## Technology-provider ecosystems

A future extension may map publicly observable relationships between institutions, software platforms, hosting services, external media platforms, standards, and technology providers.

This could support research on:

- technological dependency;
- infrastructure concentration;
- public–private relationships;
- shared platforms across institutions;
- migration between providers;
- cross-border infrastructure patterns.

Provider claims would require stronger evidence and review rules than generic technology detection.

## Comparative institutional profiles

The analytical layer can produce archive-level profiles combining access, APIs, metadata, interoperability, restrictions, and technological change.

These profiles could support:

- comparison by country;
- comparison by archive type;
- comparison between public broadcasters, film archives, museums, and aggregators;
- identification of recurring infrastructural configurations;
- transparent clustering after adequate validation and sample size.

Profiles should expose their component indicators and must not be reduced to unexplained rankings.

## Network analysis

Once entity relationships are sufficiently documented, the project may analyse networks linking:

- archives;
- aggregators;
- standards;
- platforms;
- APIs;
- providers;
- external hosting and distribution services.

Network results would require explicit edge definitions, evidence thresholds, temporal validity, and handling of unknown relationships.

## Methodological transfer

The longitudinal framework may be adapted to other institutional domains, including:

- museums;
- libraries;
- governmental repositories;
- scientific collections;
- public digital services;
- cultural heritage infrastructures.

Transfer requires new corpus policies, detector vocabularies, indicators, and validation samples. Reuse of the architecture does not imply that audiovisual-specific methods are automatically valid elsewhere.

## Research outputs

Potential future outputs include:

- versioned open datasets;
- methodological articles;
- comparative empirical articles;
- technical and scientific reports;
- archive-level profiles;
- reproducible notebooks or analysis packages;
- public dashboards;
- documented APIs;
- data releases with persistent identifiers.

## Scientific priorities

The preferred sequence is:

```text
validated observation
→ reproducible dataset
→ transparent indicator
→ longitudinal comparison
→ scientific interpretation
```

The project should avoid adding predictive models, rankings, or complex statistical analyses before the evidence base and denominator rules are sufficiently stable.

## Collaboration

The infrastructure is open to methodological exchange with researchers studying digital infrastructures, audiovisual heritage, platform governance, public access, metadata, interoperability, preservation, and computational social research.

External adaptation or collaboration should preserve the project’s core principles:

- provenance;
- explicit corpus boundaries;
- longitudinal memory;
- human review for sensitive claims;
- versioned methodology;
- transparent limitations;
- reproducibility.
